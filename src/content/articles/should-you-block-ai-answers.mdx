**The opt-out switch in Search Console is a site-wide setting that lets an owner decide whether their content is allowed to appear in Google's generative features.** Google says an opted-out site gets no visits and no impressions from them, and that the setting isn't used as a ranking signal for results outside those generative features. For now it's only being tested on a slice of sites.

The most practical fact goes first: **Google said the switch is starting out in testing with a slice of site owners in the UK**, and that it wants to make it available globally only after that. It didn't specify availability outside that test — so you may not find it in your account yet. The rest of this article is about what to do with it once it shows up.

## What the switch does, according to Google

In its June 2026 announcement, Google describes a new Search Console setting that lets a site owner decide whether their pages are allowed to appear in the generative features of Search and serve as source material for their answers — specifically in AI Overviews, in AI Mode, and in AI Overviews in Discover.

Three things from that announcement are worth remembering:

- **An opted-out site gets no visits and no impressions from the generative features.**
- **The setting isn't meant to be used as a ranking signal** for results outside those generative features; the site should stay indexed and ranked normally. But Google counts AI Overviews in Discover among the features affected.
- The control it describes works at the **whole-site level** — there's no mention of opting out only certain pages.

Alongside the switch, Google also mentions new reporting: impressions in generative features, which pages show up in AI answers and in which countries, with more metrics promised over time.

<Insight tone="key">
This isn't a fine-grained tool that lets you keep the sections that work and opt out the rest. It's one switch for the entire site — which is exactly why it can't be used as an experiment.
</Insight>

## Where it breaks down: you can't cleanly measure what the channel is worth

Availability isn't the main problem. The main problem is the decision itself.

To evaluate opting out, you'd need to know how much the generative features actually bring in and what leaving would cost. But the individual AI surfaces can't be separated out of commonly available data — Search Console reports them in aggregate, as [measuring AI Mode](/blog/measuring-ai-mode-limits/) breaks down.

<Insight tone="warning">
In practice that means you'd be **switching off a channel whose value you can't yet measure reliably** — and after switching it off, you'd have nothing to compare against, because impressions from those features simply disappear. It's a decision that's hard to make in advance and even harder to evaluate afterward.
</Insight>

## What one online store's numbers show

Our own numbers aren't representative, but they point in a direction. Our own store got **1,867 visits from AI tools over twelve months** — low in raw volume. Their conversion rate was **3.5 percent against 0.88 percent** from organic search.

That's one store, the starting point before any optimization, and visits from assistants generally rather than from Google specifically; the full data is in the [case study](/blog/case-study-ai-traffic/). Even so, it raises a question worth asking: what is a channel worth to you when it brings few people but converts above average?

## Where this article stops

**It doesn't give availability dates.** Google didn't name specific dates, and we're not going to guess at them here.

**It rests on Google's announcement, not on independent testing.** Both the description of the behavior and the reassurance about ranking are a vendor's statements about its own product.

**It won't decide for you.** The filter above is as far as anyone can go without knowing your numbers.


## What to take away

The switch is a real thing, but so far only a slice of sites has it in testing. When it arrives, it'll be one switch for the entire site.

More important than the mechanics is a question few people answer honestly: **what would you actually base the decision on?** Cleanly separating the individual AI surfaces isn't possible from commonly available data today, and after switching it off there'd be nothing to compare against. That makes it all the more important to get at least a rough estimate — how many people arrive from assistants, what they do on your site, and whether they buy — before deciding completely blind.

The line runs between sites that protect the content itself and sites that need a business result out of a visit. Which side you're on, you know better than anyone. What that channel is worth to you, though, you may not know yet — and that's something you can find out before switching anything off.
