**According to its public documentation, Google judges content mainly on quality and usefulness, not on whether a human or an AI wrote it.** The risk isn't the tool — it's mass-produced content with no added value, which is exactly what the spam policies target. This analysis pulls together what Google says publicly, where the line sits, and which qualities tend to raise or lower a page's chances of being cited in AI answers.

## What Google says publicly

In its public materials, Google says repeatedly that what counts is **the usefulness of the content, not how it was produced**. Using automation or AI is not, on its own, labeled a policy violation.

At the same time, the spam policies describe a category called **scaled content abuse**: producing large numbers of pages that give readers nothing and exist mainly to rank. What matters is the nature of the content — the same rule lands on copy written by people at volume.

<Insight tone="aio">
“Google penalizes AI content” is a simplification. More accurate: the tool doesn't matter, what matters is whether the piece is any use to anyone. That moves the question from “did AI write this?” to “is there anything here you couldn't get elsewhere?”.
</Insight>

## Why generic content has a lower chance of being cited

Citations in AI answers follow a similar logic. AI systems work in different ways: some answer from trained knowledge, others look sources up live and pick from what they find. Across many AI answers, though, **content that only repeats widely available information without adding anything tends to have a lower chance of being cited**.

Being general isn't always a problem — an overview piece gets cited too when it's clear and well structured. But without a clear answer, data, or experience, the reason to cite it is weaker. In practice, the sources with better odds are the ones with something you can't find elsewhere: [your own data](/blog/original-data-for-ai-citations/) or specific hands-on experience. More details are in the article on [how AI cites sources](/blog/how-ai-cites-sources/).

There are plenty of legitimate uses. Most of them share one thing: AI handles the prep or the routine, but the decision stays with a person.

## When it hurts

<MistakeGrid>
  <Mistake num="01" title="Mass production with no value">
    Hundreds of generated pages that give readers nothing are exactly what the spam policies describe.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake num="02" title="Unchecked facts">
    Models hallucinate — they state false information convincingly. On topics like health, finance, or law, publishing without verification is a serious risk.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake num="03" title="No first-hand experience">
    Copy with no practice behind it is just a restatement of someone else's work. The hands-on experience has to come from a person; without it, the content is interchangeable and less credible.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake num="04" title="An invented author">
    A fictional name and profile on generated copy doesn't match reality, and it's no substitute for a real author.
  </Mistake>
</MistakeGrid>

<Insight tone="warn">
Be extra careful with local specifics. Some models still produce stilted, translated-sounding phrasing, and they get local details wrong — companies, regulations, prices. A language pass and a factual check against a primary source aren't optional.
</Insight>

## What makes the difference

The difference doesn't come from the tool, it comes from what you bring. Specifically:

- **Your own data and numbers** — what the model doesn't have; see [original data for AI](/blog/original-data-for-ai-citations/).
- **Hands-on experience** — specific cases, what worked and what didn't.
- **A real author** with a findable profile; see [the author entity for AI](/blog/author-entity-for-ai/) and [E-E-A-T](/blog/e-e-a-t-for-ai/) (experience, expertise, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness).
- **Verified facts** — numbers, names, and quotes above all.
- **Freshness** — see [content updates for AI](/blog/content-updates-for-ai/).
- **Structure** — so the answer can be found; see [content for AI](/blog/ai-seo-content/).

## The takeaway

- **The tool itself isn't the deciding factor** — per Google's public statements, what counts is usefulness, not how the content was made.
- **The risk is scale without value** — that's what the spam policies target, for people and AI alike.
- **Generic content has a lower chance of being cited** — without a clear answer, data, or experience, the reason to link to it is weaker.
- **Your input makes the difference** — your own data, experience, verified facts, a real author.
- **The question isn't “did AI write this?”** — it's whether there's anything in the piece you couldn't get elsewhere.
