**Passage-level writing means writing each section so it makes sense even when it's pulled off the page.** Every section gets its own heading that names its topic specifically, the answer in the first sentence, and no dependence on the surrounding copy: no backward references like “as we mentioned above,” no pronouns without a named subject, and abbreviations spelled out where they're used.

The reason is mundane. Only a piece of a page often gets used — whether it's read by a person who landed straight in the middle from a link, or by a system assembling an answer from several sources.

## What breaks a passage

It's almost always one of six things. What they have in common is that they lean on something that isn't in the section itself.

## The cover test

<Insight tone="aio">
Cover everything except one section and read it. **If you have to scroll up to understand it, the passage isn't self-contained.** It's a crude check, but it catches most of the problems in a few minutes — and it needs no tools.
</Insight>

Work through the page this way, section by section. A common pattern: the first stretch of copy holds up and the problems pile up toward the end, where the writer was already going full speed.

## Example: before and after the rewrite

Nothing shows it faster than an example. This section leans hard on the surrounding context:

> ### Additional notes
>
> As we mentioned above, the same goes for it. And per the table above it's cheaper, so it usually pays off. CTR doesn't change.

Pulled off the page it says nothing at all: we don't know what it applies to, what's cheaper, or what CTR is. After the rewrite:

> ### When annual billing pays off
>
> Annual billing on the Standard plan pays off if you use the service for more than seven months. It works out 18% cheaper than paying month to month. Click-through rate (CTR) isn't affected either way.

Five things changed: the heading names the section's topic specifically, the first sentence answers, named entities replaced the vague pronouns, the number sits in the copy instead of pointing at a table, and the abbreviation is spelled out. The length stayed almost the same.

## What to avoid

<MistakeGrid>
  <Mistake num="01" title="Headings with no content">
    “More information” or “Note” say nothing about what's in the section. A heading should carry the question the section answers.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake num="02" title="Unanchored pronouns">
    “It,” “that one,” “this” — in a passage pulled off the page there's nothing to attach them to. Name the subject.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake num="03" title="Abbreviation only at the top of the page">
    Anyone landing straight into a section in the middle never sees the definition in the intro. Spell it out where you use it, too.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake num="04" title="Overdone self-containment">
    Repeating the full context in every section makes the copy robotic. The goal is clarity, not a copy of the intro in every paragraph.
  </Mistake>
</MistakeGrid>

<Insight tone="warn">
That last point is worth stressing, because it gets overlooked. When every paragraph restates what the whole article is about, you end up with copy that reads worse than the original. **Add enough context that the section makes sense — no more.** Usually it's enough to name the subject, spell out the abbreviation, and give the table a caption.
</Insight>

## Where this approach has limits

**It doesn't apply to every genre.** In narrative pieces, case studies, or articles built on an idea unfolding step by step, continuity is the point, and breaking it would do damage. Passage-level writing is mainly for how-tos, roundups, comparisons, and documentation.

**It doesn't guarantee a citation.** It's craft that raises the odds a section is usable, not a visibility switch. It fixes form, not substance: a self-contained passage with nothing of value in it is still empty.

**It isn't only “for AI.”** Readers scan, and they arrive from links into the middle of a page. Copy that holds up in pieces serves them better regardless of who's reading it.

## The takeaway

Pages rarely get read end to end. A reader arrives from a link into the middle, scans two headings, and leaves; a system may work with a single section. Copy that assumes a top-to-bottom read is the copy that loses.

The craft is cheap: a specific heading, the answer in the first sentence, a named subject, a spelled-out abbreviation. Plus the cover test, which takes a few minutes and shows you where things fall apart.

Just don't overdo it. The goal is a section you can understand on its own — not an article that repeats itself in every paragraph.
