## What <span class="hl">2026</span> <strong>actually changes</strong> for online stores

Two years ago, SEO for an online store came down to three layers: **technical health**, **on-page optimization** of categories and product pages, and **off-page** in the form of backlinks. Those three layers still work in 2026 — without them you'll typically lose ground on indexing, relevance and organic competitiveness. What has **changed fundamentally** is the economics of the click.

Your customer now searches in **three different modes**, often all in the same day:

1. **Classic Google search** — blue links, category pages, product pages. This is where you fight for positions 1–10, the way we're used to.
2. **Google's AI Overviews** — a generated summary above the results that usually assembles an answer from several sources and, for a share of queries, reduces the user's motivation to click through. It fires mainly on informational queries (“which cordless drill for an apartment”, “road running shoes vs. trail”).
3. **ChatGPT, Perplexity, Claude, Gemini** — generative AI tools where the user simply types “recommend a laptop under $1,200 for graphic design work” and expects a specific answer with links.

**The gap between the channels that bring you traffic and the ones that don't is what matters in 2026.** For an online store that has only ever worried about Google rankings, that means adding **two new questions** to classic SEO: where it pays to make your content easier for machines to read and cite (so AI tools quote you), and where you should instead protect valuable content from zero-click loss (so AI Overviews don't take the click on educational queries). This isn't a new discipline separate from SEO — it's a new context for the classic content/IA/structured-data playbook.

### 1. FAQ sections on categories with a research phase

A well-written FAQ section turns a category page into better-structured, more citable content for both search engines and AI tools. `FAQPage` schema is technical hygiene — Google has scaled back the impact of FAQ rich results in recent years, so the schema alone guarantees neither a rich snippet nor a citation in AI Overviews. The priority is to **write questions from real customer queries and phrase the answers clearly, self-containedly and factually** — the schema is just the technical wrapper.

Example: a “cordless drills” category with FAQ entries like “which drill for home use” or “hammer drill vs. cordless drill” — pull the answers from **People Also Ask** in Google, from **customer reviews**, and from **support tickets**.

**What to do:**
- Take the top 20 categories on your site → the ones whose products have a research phase get an FAQ section with 4–6 questions
- Validate the structured data with [Google Rich Results Test](https://search.google.com/test/rich-results)

### 2. Product schema with reviews, price and availability

`schema.org/Product` is a technical priority for an online store because it helps search engines unambiguously read a product's price, availability, brand, rating and variants. On its own it **guarantees neither Shopping visibility nor AI citations** — a clean Merchant Center feed, correct GTIN/EAN, availability, reviews and a competitive offer matter just as much for product results. But without valid Product schema you're denying the search engine a clean machine-readable input for price and availability, which weakens your odds of rich results across the board.

**What Product schema must contain:**
- `name`, `image`, `description`
- `brand` (the manufacturer's brand)
- `offers` with `price`, `priceCurrency` and `availability`
- `aggregateRating` with `ratingValue` and `reviewCount` (if you have reviews)
- a `review` array with specific customer reviews

Most e-commerce platforms (Shopify, WooCommerce with an SEO plugin, BigCommerce) generate Product schema automatically, but **the quality of the generated markup varies**. An audit through Rich Results Test is 30 minutes of work and often reveals that the schema is technically valid but missing the fields that matter in the AI era.

### 3. A short intro above the product listing

A classic category page opens with a grid of products and puts the SEO copy at the bottom. **For categories with a research phase that isn't ideal** — neither the user nor the search engine quickly learns what the category contains and how to choose.

The principle isn't a fixed character limit but clarity: above the listing there should be **2–4 sentences (~40–60 words)** saying what the category contains, who it's for, and how to choose. That helps users, search engines and AI tools quickly grasp the point of the category.

**The right category format for 2026:**

1. H1 — the category name
2. **A short answer block (40–60 words)** — what the category covers, who it's for, what the main variables are (in bold)
3. 2–3 paragraphs of educational copy (how to choose, what to watch out for)
4. **The product grid**
5. **An FAQ section** with 4–6 questions (with FAQPage schema)
6. Longer SEO copy at the bottom (optional)

This format covers **classic SEO** (copy up top = relevance), **AI Overviews** (FAQ + a short answer = citation hook) and **the conversion path** (the product grid right after the answer block, no scrolling).

### 4. Trust and transparency signals

[E-E-A-T](/blog/e-e-a-t-for-ai/) (Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trustworthiness) **is not a standalone ranking factor** — it's a framework from Google's Quality Rater Guidelines for judging experience, expertise, authority and trustworthiness, which the algorithm approximates through various proxy signals. For online stores it translates in practice into trust and transparency signals, which always matter — regardless of whether Google explicitly calls them out as a priority for your product category today.

**What that means for an online store in practice:**
- **Reviews and ratings** — displayed directly on the product page, not just on third-party review platforms
- **Visibly updated, consistent content** (for advisory content on a store — blog posts and buying guides — `dateModified` in Article schema works; for product entities it's more about keeping availability, price and reviews current)
- **An author for the product copy** — ideally with a name and credentials (e.g. “tested by our product team”)
- **Transparent company information** — address, business registration details, contact info, terms of service, privacy policy
- **Brand mentions** in authoritative sources — review platforms, industry blogs, podcasts

For a small store the easiest win is to **collect reviews systematically** (an email after every order asking for a rating) and connect them to Product schema. Most platforms can do this automatically — it just has to be switched on.

### 5. A defensive strategy for educational content

If your store runs a blog or “tips and how-tos” categories, **AI Overviews are taking that traffic**. The user types “how to store wine”, gets a short summary at the top of Google, and never clicks through to your guide.

**Three ways to respond:**

1. **Embrace** — accept that AI Overviews will cite you (= brand exposure without the click) and optimize for citation rate. Right if the goal is visibility, not direct click-through.
2. **Bypass** — explicitly block Google on specific blocks with the `data-nosnippet` HTML attribute. **An advanced tactic with trade-offs** — it can reduce snippet appeal for classic featured snippets too, so it's only worth testing on selected content types (long-form guides where all the value is in the click). For most online stores, differentiating the content beats blanket blocking. More details are in [How to turn off AI Overviews](/blog/how-to-turn-off-ai-overviews/).
3. **Hybrid** — a short FAQ block at the top (open to AI Overviews), long detailed content below (with `data-nosnippet` on the key reveals). A balanced option for stores with a large advisory section, not a universal best practice — it needs an A/B test on a selected segment of URLs.

More details are in the separate [guide to limiting AI Overviews](/blog/how-to-turn-off-ai-overviews/) — see the section on blocking AI Overviews on your own site.

## <strong>What it costs</strong> — <span class="hl">how the tiers break down</span>

Agency pricing for e-commerce SEO falls into roughly three tiers. The scale below is **relative, not a price list** — the actual numbers vary enormously by market, platform, URL count, implementation scope and the seniority of the vendor:

| What | Relative cost | Timeline |
|---|---:|---|
| **Quick entry-level SEO check** (mini audit of title/meta, indexability, top problems) | lowest tier | days |
| **Full SEO audit** (technical + on-page + off-page + prioritized roadmap) | several times a mini audit | 2–4 weeks |
| **Implementation work** (audit + actually implementing the priority fixes) | scales with scope | 2–6 weeks |
| **Page Speed / Core Web Vitals** (technical focus) | comparable to a full audit | 2–4 weeks |
| **Content audit** (categories + product pages + copywriting plan) | comparable to a full audit | 2–3 weeks |
| **Ongoing SEO retainer** (monthly) | the largest recurring line item | ongoing |

**What the price usually includes:**
- A diagnostic report with prioritized findings
- Specific recommendations with an estimate of impact and effort
- Implementation support (the implementation itself is often billed separately)
- A re-audit after implementation (confirming the fix actually landed)

**What the price usually does NOT include:**
- Paid tool subscriptions (Ahrefs, Semrush — billed separately, per seat)
- Content production (category and product copy, blog)
- Link building campaigns
- A/B testing of conversion elements

If you're considering buying SEO services, **don't sign a long-term contract without an audit first**. A one-time audit shows whether the vendor's scope and priorities make sense for your site. The payback on the audit itself depends on your implementation capacity — without follow-up implementation an audit stays a diagnostic document.

## When to keep it <span class="hl">in-house</span> and when to <strong>go with an agency</strong>

Not every online store needs an agency. For some, the owner's time is the better investment; for others, doing it in-house is simply inefficient. Here's the framework we use as a decision matrix:

<Compare>
  <CompareCol heading="In-house makes sense" tone="seo" subtitle="When you have time and a simple site">
    - A store with **under 100 products** and 5 main categories
    - The owner or a content manager has **2–4 hours a week** for SEO
    - The platform is **a standard Shopify or WooCommerce setup** with no custom work
    - You're targeting **classic organic search**, not AI visibility
    - You have **basic SEO knowledge** or the willingness to learn
  </CompareCol>
  <CompareCol heading="Consider an agency" tone="aeo" subtitle="When you want to grow faster">
    - **500+ products** and more than 10 categories
    - You want to grow **faster than the slow organic route allows**
    - You need **custom modules** or a platform migration
    - You want **AI visibility** (AI Overviews citations, ChatGPT recommendations)
    - Strong competition in your niche (the top 10 is dominated by marketplaces and major retailers)
    - **Time-to-result** is critical (you're hiring know-how, not learning)
  </CompareCol>
</Compare>

For borderline cases (200–500 products), a **hybrid strategy** works well: an agency audit + roadmap → the owner implements gradually → the agency reviews results and updates the strategy quarterly. A fraction of the cost of a full ongoing retainer.
