## AI SEO content: four principles of citable copy

**AI SEO content is copy written so that AI search and answer systems can easily understand it and cite it.** It rests on four principles: the answer up front, high fact density, original data or named methods, and verifiable claims backed by sources.

This is the **copywriting layer** of AI SEO — how to write an individual passage. Site architecture and the links between pages belong to [pillar content structure](/blog/how-to-structure-pillar-content/); here the goal is for every paragraph to yield a self-contained, citable chunk.

## What AI looks for in your copy

AI search and answer systems assemble a result from the passages they find and judge to be relevant and trustworthy. So alongside classic SEO signals, what matters more is whether a specific claim can be pulled from your copy **quickly and verifiably**.

In practice, cited passages share three traits: the answer is up top, the content is dense with facts, and the claims are backed by evidence. Treat specific numbers from published analyses as rough guidance — this is a practical rule for editing copy, not a universally measured ranking factor.

## Principle 1: The answer up front

AI and your reader both want the answer immediately, not after three paragraphs of throat-clearing:

<Checklist tone="aeo" title="The answer up front" items={[
  { title: "A short answer up top", desc: "40–60 words under the heading that stands on its own." },
  { title: "A direct answer in every section", desc: "the first 1–2 sentences answer, the rest expands." },
  { title: "Headings as questions", desc: "where it makes sense — they mirror how people ask AI." }
]} />

It's the same principle as [answer engine optimization (AEO)](/answer-engine-optimization/), and it holds across [ChatGPT](/blog/chatgpt-seo/) and [Perplexity](/blog/perplexity-seo/) alike.

## Principle 2: High fact density

One of the most visible differences between a usable passage and a weak one is <span class="hl">fact density</span> — how many verifiable claims you fit into a given length of copy.

- Add a **specific number, date, example, or name** roughly every 80–150 words.
- Replace vague phrases (“significantly improves”) with specific figures (“cuts load time by 1.2 s”).
- Denser content is easier to use as a source than generic phrasing — specific facts give AI clearer material to work with.

Fact density doesn't mean padding — the point is that every claim carries something you can back up.

## Principle 3: Original data and named methods

Retelling widely available information is hard to distinguish as a source. A fact, comparison, or method that isn't available elsewhere is stronger:

- **Your own number or comparison** from your work or your data.
- **A simple chart or table** built on your own figures.
- **A named method or framework** (for example, “the 4-layer audit rule”) that others can point to.

That also demonstrates experience under **E‑E‑A‑T** (experience, expertise, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness) — something a generic AI summary doesn't have, and something that raises a source's credibility.

## Principle 4: Evidence and sources

In systems that assemble an answer from retrieved passages, verifiable claims have a better shot at holding up as a source:

## Which formats get cited more often

Some formats are easier to pull from than running prose. Frequently cited ones include:

- **Clean lists** (numbered and bulleted).
- **Tables** with clear rows and columns.
- **Comparison articles** in the “X vs Y” format, which answer a decision-stage query directly.

That doesn't mean writing everything as a table — the point is to offer structure that an answer can easily be pulled from.

## What's next: put it to work on your own site

Citable content is where form (answer up top, fact density) meets credibility (data, sources, experience). Start with the pages that already earn the most traffic: move the answer to the top, add a specific fact every 80–150 words, and cite a source for every number you use.
